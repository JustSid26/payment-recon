01

What reconciliation actually is

Reconciliation is the practice of taking two independent records of the same money and proving they agree — then explaining every difference until none is left unexplained.

A single card payment is recorded by many parties: the cardholder's bank, the card network, the acquiring bank, the gateway, and the merchant. Each keeps its own ledger. None of them trusts the others blindly. Reconciliation is the discipline of comparing these records and asking one question over and over:

◆ The only question
“Does the money I expected match the money that actually moved — and if not, exactly why?”

Every unexplained difference is called a break (or exception). Recon isn't done when the totals match by luck — it's done when every break has a documented reason: a fee you didn't account for, a payout that's still in transit, a chargeback, an FX difference, a duplicate, a genuine error. Good reconciliation turns "the numbers are off by €412" into "the numbers are off by €412 because of these 6 specific items, and here's what to do about each."

Source A

What our own system says happened (our ledger).

Source B

What the counterparty says happened (their statement / file).

The breaks

▸ The fee stack, decoded
Interchange goes to the issuing bank (largest slice, set by the networks). Scheme fees go to Visa/Mastercard. Acquirer markup is the acquiring bank's cut. Together these are the gateway's cost of acceptance. The MDR (Merchant Discount Rate) is what the gateway charges the merchant — and the gateway's real profit is MDR − (interchange + scheme + acquirer), not the whole MDR.

Two economic truths fall out of this diagram, and they define the two halves of the system:

on the right

Gateway owes the merchant

You captured €100, you'll keep your MDR + fees + a rolling reserve, and you owe the merchant the net. This is a payable.

on the left

The bank owes the gateway

The acquirer collected the €100, will keep interchange + scheme + its markup, and owes you the rest. This is a receivable.

03

The two reconciliation frontiers

This platform sits in the middle of the pipe, so it has two edges to reconcile. Today one edge is fully built; the other is the roadmap.

✓ BUILT

Gateway → Merchant

“Did we pay each merchant exactly what we owe them?”

  • Import processor transaction reports
  • Book MDR, per-txn fees, rolling reserve
  • Net into settlement statements (T+N)
  • Pay out & produce a per-transaction breakdown
  • Reconcile: capture − fees − reserve = payout ✓
◷ ROADMAP

Bank → Gateway

“Did the acquiring bank fund us exactly what we're owed?”

  • Import the acquirer's settlement file
  • Decompose MDR into interchange / scheme / acquirer cost
  • Match expected funding vs actual deposit
  • Raise & work breaks (missing, variance, timing, FX)
  • Reconcile: what we expected = what the bank paid ✓
◆ Why they feel different
The merchant side generates truth — the ledger decides what's owed and pays it. The bank side compares two truths — your ledger vs the bank's file — and surfaces disagreements. The first is a settlement engine; the second is a matching engine.
04

The double-entry ledger — why any of this is trustworthy

Reconciliation is only as good as your own source of truth. This system's source of truth is a double-entry, append-only ledger — and that design is what makes it reconcilable at all.

▸ Double-entry
Every event is recorded as balanced legs — debits and credits that sum to exactly zero. Money is never created or destroyed, only moved between accounts. A database trigger rejects any event that doesn't balance.
▸ Append-only
Rows are never updated or deleted — triggers forbid it. A correction is a new reversing event. This means the ledger is a permanent audit trail: you can always replay exactly how a balance got to where it is.

05

The built workflow, step by step gateway → merchant

This is the full pipeline that runs today, from a raw processor file to a merchant payout with a reconciled statement.

  1. Import the processor reports

    admin.py · importer.py

    Admin uploads the gateway's transaction CSVs (and the settlement workbook). Each row becomes a transactions record. Deduplication is on (merchant_id, upstream_payment_id) — re-uploading the same file posts nothing.

  2. Quarantine unknown merchants

    importer.py → known_rates()

    If we don't hold a real rate card for a merchant, they're imported but quarantined (ledger_posted = false) — no ledger events, no assumed 5% MDR. Nothing is booked until someone assigns a fee schedule. This prevents inventing revenue for merchants we haven't priced.

  3. Onboard & assign fees

    onboarding.py → assign_fee_schedule()

    Assigning a schedule (or a saved preset like Workbook/Canamoney) activates the merchant and replays their quarantined transactions into real ledger events — atomically, in one transaction.

  4. Book the economics on each capture

    importer.py → _post_transaction()

    For every capture: deduct MDR + approved fee (→ gateway_revenue), credit the merchant's payable, and hold a rolling reserve. Declines may carry a decline fee; refunds reverse principal + a refund fee. Each event is stamped with a settle_after date = occurred + T+N delay.

  5. Run the settlement cycle

    cycle.py → run_cycle() · settle.py → generate()

    On a chosen cutoff, the engine sweeps every unsettled payable event with settle_after ≤ cutoff, nets them per merchant×currency, deducts the settlement fee, and creates a settlement. Deficits (net ≤ 0) are skipped and carry forward. This is idempotent per cutoff date.

  6. Pay out & complete

    settle.py → complete()

    The settled batch moves settlement_payable → clearing with a payout reference — the simulated bank rail. (Wiring a real SEPA/USDC rail is the only missing piece here.)

  7. Release rolling reserves when due

    cycle.py → release_due_reserves()

    Holds whose release_due_at has arrived are released back to payable (a reserve_release event) so they settle in that cycle — the reserve was insurance against chargebacks that never came.

  8. Produce the statement & per-transaction breakdown

    settle.py → line_items() · SettlementDetail.tsx

    The merchant (and admin) see a settlement statement and a per-transaction view showing the exact charges on each payment — gross, MDR, fees, reserve, net — exportable to CSV / Excel, with the statement printable to PDF.

06

Where reconciliation lives in the built side

On the merchant side, the ledger is the truth, so recon is mostly about proving the ledger's internal consistency and that each statement ties out.

Internal integrity checks

Every event balances to zero (enforced), the ledger is append-only (enforced), and account balances equal the sum of their entries. The Integrity page surfaces these continuously — if any invariant broke, you'd see it.

Idempotency = dedup recon

Two idempotency keys prevent double-counting: (merchant_id, upstream_payment_id) on transactions, and (source_type, source_id) on ledger postings. Re-importing or re-running a cycle can't post the same money twice.

The statement tie-out

A merchant settlement reconciles by construction:

◆ The identity that must hold
gross captured − MDR − txn fees − refunds − reserve held + reserve released − settlement fee = net payout

The per-transaction export exists precisely so a recon person (or the merchant) can drill from that one net number down to the individual payments behind it, and confirm each charge. The line items sum to the statement subtotal; the settlement fee is the one statement-level charge on top.

⚠ The honest gap
Today the merchant side reconciles against itself. It does not yet reconcile against the bank — so it can't tell you whether the money you paid merchants actually arrived from the acquirer, or whether the acquirer's fees matched what you assumed. That's the entire point of the next section.
07

The bank → gateway side roadmap

This is the missing half: reconciling what the acquiring bank actually funds you against what your own ledger says you're owed. It's additive — new accounts and new events, nothing to rewrite.

◆ The one central refactor
Right now clearing is a magic infinite bucket silently doing the bank's job. The whole bank-side build is really about splitting clearing into real accounts: acquirer_receivable (what the bank owes us), gateway_bank (cash that actually landed), and the cost accounts. Once money-in-transit is explicit, everything else follows.

What you add, in order

  1. New ledger accounts (the bank leg)

    acquirer_receivable, gateway_bank/nostro, and the cost accounts interchange_expense, scheme_fee_expense, acquirer_fee_expense (plus fx_gain_loss if the bank funds in a different currency, and optionally an acquirer-held reserve).

  2. Decompose the MDR into real cost

    Today gateway_revenue = the full MDR, which overstates profit. Split each capture's cost into interchange + scheme + acquirer, so true margin = MDR − those costs becomes a real, per-transaction number.

  3. Model acquirers & MIDs

    A gateway routes through several acquiring banks. Your transactions.mid field already exists — add an acquirers table mapping MID → acquirer, each with its own currency, funding timing (bank T+1/T+2), and cost schedule.

  4. Ingest the acquirer settlement file

    The mirror of your processor importer: the bank's daily settlement report → acquirer_settlements (batch header: gross, fees, net, funding date, reference) + acquirer_settlement_items (links to your transactions).

  5. Run the matching engine

    For each batch, compare expected (Σ captures − expected interchange/scheme/acquirer) against actual (what the bank deposited). Produce breaks. — detailed next section.

  6. Work the breaks

    An exception workflow: unmatched items with statuses (matched / unmatched / disputed / written-off), notes, assignment. This screen is where a recon analyst spends their day.

  7. Unlock true reporting

    Real per-transaction margin, a treasury/cash position (receivable from acquirer vs cash funded vs owed to merchants), and funding-timing exposure — the working capital gap when you pay merchants T+0 but get funded T+2.

⚠ The hard part, named honestly
Real interchange is a giant matrix (card type × region × MCC × more). Don't model it fully on day one — start with a blended rate per acquirer and refine. The matching engine and break workflow are where the actual value is; the fee tables can stay coarse for a long time.
08

The matching engine, explained

Matching is the mechanical heart of bank reconciliation: line up two lists of money and classify every line as matched or as a specific kind of break.

How lines get matched

Each transaction in your ledger and each line in the acquirer's file get a match key — typically the payment/ARN reference, or (amount + date + MID) when no shared ID exists. The engine joins on the key, then compares amounts within a tolerance (rounding, FX). Whatever doesn't cleanly match becomes a break.

The gateway charged the merchant 6.85 (MDR + fee) but its real cost of acceptance was 2.15. True gross margin on this payment = €4.70 — a figure you literally cannot compute until both reconciliation frontiers exist. That's the payoff of building the bank side.
10

Glossary

Acquirer / Acquiring bank
The bank that holds the gateway's merchant account and funds the gateway for captured payments.
Issuer / Issuing bank
The cardholder's bank. Receives interchange.
Interchange
Fee paid to the issuer, set by the card networks. Usually the biggest cost slice.
Scheme fee
Assessment paid to Visa/Mastercard for using their network.
MDR (Merchant Discount Rate)
The blended % the gateway charges the merchant. Covers all the above plus the gateway's margin.
MID
Merchant ID at the acquirer — identifies which bank account/route a transaction settled through.
Capture
Finalising an authorised payment so the money can actually move.
Settlement
Netting a batch of transactions into a single amount owed, then paying it.
Clearing account
A holding account for money in transit. Here, currently a stand-in for the whole bank leg.
Rolling reserve
A % of each payout held back for a period, as insurance against future chargebacks/refunds.
Chargeback
A cardholder-initiated forced reversal; the acquirer debits the gateway, who debits the merchant.
T+N
Funds settle N business days after the transaction. T+0 = same day; T+2 = two days later.
Nostro
The gateway's own cash account at a bank (“our money at their bank”).
Break / Exception
An unexplained difference between two records. The output of reconciliation.
Idempotency
A guarantee that doing the same operation twice has the same effect as once — no double-counting.
Double-entry
Every event recorded as balanced debits and credits summing to zero.
